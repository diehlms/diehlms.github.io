I was talking with a friend about their whoop watch. By now, whoops have been on the marketplace for some time, and they look similar to fitbits, or like a slim garmin. They have no visible interface or interaction surface, but they collect vast amounts of data similar to other wearable health gadgits.

My friend was talking about how to get started with his whoop. First, he documented his self-audited sleep patterns, including how much he drank that day, how much he worked out, his general mood, and how many times he had sex in the past week or so. Ostensibly, the data he was volunteering to his device was in service of gathering some report of why he had been sleeping so poorly. For instance, if a graph showed every day he had sex, didn't drink, worked out no more than 45 minutes, and read 20 pages of a book he had a great night of sleep, that might lead to some interesting recommendations.

It was hard for me to square away my feelings about documenting such private information and data about oneself to some faceless corp. On the surface, there is an obvious nefarious quality to the way data about my sex life and alcohol use could be abused. But rather, the thing I left wondering about is the purpose of the data itself. My thesis is somewhere in the realm of: if you simply write down the fact that you sleep poorly and a few notes about your day, it wouldnt take long before you can reread this journal or diary or log and make a pretty good conclusion about what may or may not be contributing to your bad sleep. After all, you are the author and maintainer of the information itself, and therefore have no reason to withhold the truth about your life, apart from shame I suppose.

One of the takeaways from Michael Pollen's 'In Defense of Food' I got was to not overthink nutrition. There is an enormous industrial apperatus out there to treat the way we eat as something that can be hacked, or optimized in such a way that you can finally be free of your pain, your endless suffering. How great it would be. The industry hides the complexities of our bodies, which are numerous. What may be good for you may not be good for me. Somethings are almost universally good (sweet potatos), while others are almost universally bad (twinkies). But the complexities are found on the edges; how much Omega5 should I be consuming? Am I getting enough zinc? Or calcium? It's possible these questions poke at the truth of what ails us, but they are not the truthful questions. Our nutrition can be simple and beautiful as Pollen puts it: "dont eat too much, and eat green stuff" (my paraphrasing).

Our health data can be simple and beautiful more generally, Move around a lot, doing stuff you enjoy. Run, swim, or bike a lot, if you like to do those things. But everything in balance. What the health data we give our gadgets does not say about us speaks volumes. My watch never tells me if I enjoyed my exercise, but it gives me my adjust V02 max, or GAP pace. It is an overcomplication, a distraction of the real value of movement which is to be the best of our own bodies.de
