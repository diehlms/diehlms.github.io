I have often thought of myself as the dumb one of the bunch. Assignming myself as the dumb one has carried its way from as early as I can remember, and continues through my present employment. The essay here is not a cry for help, or an invitation to a pity party. The words here are not a flogging, a public display of self deprecation. From conversations I've had with loved ones, coworkers, and strangers, I hope nothing I say is controversial in any way and serves only as a musing on what it means to be alive now with just enough knowledge equipped to reckon with the vastness of intelligent space.
<br/><br/>
It is wholly impossible to measure dumbness. Dumbness is different than anti-intelligence, which is also impossible to measure though we as humans can't help but try through rituals like IQ tests. Wheras intelligence is possibly some quality of how quickly information leads to knowledge or positive reinforcement, dumbness is a static variable. To be dumb is mortal. To be dumb is inescapable, a force of cosmic goo which grips the ankles and holds on for eternity against the wriggling struggle of those who are dumb and wish not to be.
<br/><br/>
I want to take the sting out of being dumb, as there is much joy in the dumb brain. Dumbness is not a function of accomplishment. Dumb people do big and great things all the time; through dumbness anything is possible. Take me. Feeling dumb is walking amongst others, being impressed by them. I enjoy being on the bus with what look like many adults, effortlessly shuffling from their homes to their work, conducting calls or their own personal business with skill. I know, I know, I know. Inside the head of all these commuters lives a different, darker reality. My dumb brain tries to play catch up to all these people.
<br></br>
One of, if not the most cliched diagnoses of the current time is "Imposter Syndrome". I can't be bothered to look up the origins, but for the unaware Imposter Syndrome is the anxiety associated with one's perceived professional/social/educational inabilities or lack of skill which a sufferer feels is sure to be brought to light any day now, regardless of any external signals to the contrary. Like most cliches it contains a rich nugget of genuine truth. The truth is people who have "Imposter Syndrome" are not actually dumb, it is instead many of us think we're dumb.
<br/><br/>
I refrain from saying I have "Imposter Syndrome"; it feels similar in spirit to the idea of the "Ambivert", or someone who is neither an extravert nor an introvert: a term borne of finally avoiding saying something definitive. But the truth I can't hide is my world makes me feel dumb.
<br/><br/>
Attention is our most valuable asset and currency, and a rich investment in getting attention is to learn to be loud and proud. Once mastered, the can be quite the oyster. Our phones, the ads on every canvasable surface, the buskers on the street are trading their hardened skills for one spec of our precious attention. When our attention is given so freely our eyes get flooded with reminders of how beautiful some people's voices are, how knowledgable others are on how the world functions, and just how much money there is to go around.
<br/><br/>
It is an awesome feeling to submit to the dumb brain and casually watch in silence.
<br/><br/>
My profession is software engineering. I go to my job to write code and talk with people about what they want to do with software I help craft. The advent of LLMs and agentic programming have changed the relationship in style but not necessarily in function. A software engineer if they are worth their weight is going to have to do lots of question asking, clarifying, refinement of goals, and assess quality of varying degrees of tangiblity. Superficially, the "look" of software engineering is clacking away at a keyboard using a monospace font. When I first started coding and programming, it was valuable to know a lot of stuff. It was valuable to know the niches of the library or language you were using or writing with; knowing more meant being able to go faster and deliver something to someone sooner than someone else.
<br/><br/>
I think the days of defining value that way are over, and have been for a while. Much blog writing has been spilled hashing this same old story again and again. Knowing the technical minutae of a language or library is just only so helpful now that the robots know it all and can regurgitate it faster. And I know the counterpoints about the product an LLM delivers being only superficially good; underneath the shiny surfact is a lot of crap. But our knowledge of numpy, or python's garbage collection was superficial already.
<br/><br/>
Regardless, the firehose of visably functional stuff an LLM pumps out compared to what I can do with my own two hands is at times overpowering. But it's not what you know, it's how to think as the adage goes. Then also, it has become time to learn how to architect software from spec; by defining the direction of engineering and system functionalty for the machine, the software engineer keeps his day job. How long will this be the high water mark for the employed and employeable? What are the dumb to do once the tide rises even more?
<br/><br/>
There was an [Atlantic article](https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/2026/03/intelligence-concept/686121/) I read recently asking the nature of what being intelligent means, or meant before AI, and how we apply it on top of these AI machines. The thesis goes roughly like: these machines only mimic real intelligence using some sort of stochastic method, but can't create intelligence out of thin air the way humans can. It's an argument that get's brought up again and again, and the thinking drives me insane. Why is mimicking intelligence not intelligent; is it because mimicking is not creative or destructive? Does it not stand to reason smart people create brilliant things frequently by regurgitating and repetition of honed skills, and then suddenly a novel approach bores itself out. What I find dumb about our use of AI technology is the way we're holding it. We have a box of all intelligence ready to go, and our wielding of it is to make a chatbot, and do our jobs, the same jobs, the same way we've been doing jobs, but faster? We the dumb and brilliant are still so early on this dumb, brilliant technology.
