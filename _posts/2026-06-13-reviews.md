I attended a friend's wedding a few years ago. Keeping a long story somewhat short, the bride and her brother had hatched a plan for him to propose to his girlfriend right after the ceremony. The plan was for the brother to take the flowers during the bouquet toss while the bridesmaids would step back for the brother to take a knee. It was well orchestrated, the plan went off with just the right hitch in place, and luckily for all of us it was caught on video via an attendee's cell phone. It was posted online, and at some point soon thereafter went mildly viral. For the record, and to reiterate, the entire plan was done with full consent of the bride, groom, and bridesmaids, and the girlfriend was enthused and totally on board.

The point of the story was the viralness of it, specifically the comments I read. There was no shortage of comments excoriating the brother for how selfish it was to steal the spotlight from the bride. I'm not sure it's fair to say the response was typical, but it wasn't abnormal judging. But I was there at the wedding in the flesh. I was with everybody involved in the viral vignette. I knew the backstory, and I knew why they orchestrated the entire ruse in the first place. I reflect on this episode quite a bit when I confront online discourse, especially when it bleeds offline, I find steeped in judgement. The online commenters knew nothing as they weren't there, and I knew basically everything. But if I didn't know everything about the story, why wouldn't my brain be swayed by the judgement of my internet peers? More prescient, what am I currently being swayed by to where I have none of the requisite prior knowledge? Why do we need the opinions of people we don't know to provide us information they themselves do not know?

iI was talking with my parents a couple of years ago about a promotional feature a now defunct Airline presented frequent fliers. For a reasonable cost, you could purchase a vacation package which included hotel stays, return airline trips, and one or two other nice to haves with the caveat being you, the purchaser of the package, will not know where you are going until you show up to the airport for your flight. I am not sure how much nudging the company would give you in terms of what to pack, what to expect, or if there were any opt-outs with regards to cities which you may be attempting to never go back to.

The concept of jetting off to somewhere, anywhere, is such a romantic and instagram-able bit of consumer behavior it remains the only scene in “Yes Man” starring Jim Carry that I remember. So why aren't these promotional packages the hot new thing to go viral? My vibe tells me the typical interaction with travel, discourse, and the day to day behaviorisms of of consumption is behind some kind of “review-wall”.  The “review-wall” is a shield which protects, contains, and therefore traps against any sort of experimentation with our localisms. The “review-wall” is a muzzle disguised as a megaphone, and through no fault of anyone in particular is likely driving most people just a little mad.

To understand the “review-wall”, go to Google Maps (or Apple Maps, etc). The \Map\ apps provide a forum to review anything, from cafes to natural landmarks. Everything on a map app exists to be judged and rated. These forums are completely public and anyone with an opinion is invited to blast their review out to the world. If a cafe serves you the greatest latte you’ve ever enjoyed, you are often encouraged to post your review on the \Map\ app of your choosing, so other latte enthusiasts can share the experience you’ve just had. Review sites like the \Map\ apps, and Yelp before them, are outstandingly valuable resources for small businesses, which thrive and perish on the backs of upswells of good or bad reviews; that is to say, the “review-wall” is a valuable service with real impact on the way we interact with our communities. One time, after dining in a restaurant and paying for our food, my friends and I shuffled outside. While we are lining up to take a group photo, one of the chefs came outside, took my friends phone, silently went to google maps, and implicitely asking for a high rating showed an un-filled out input form for a review of his restaurant. It was a driven attempt to shake us down for an opinion but it was hard to fault him for his tenacity. 

The biggest downside to everyone reviewing everything is the experience I have of being in the same place everywhere. If feels the inevitable outcome of a restaurant is to become Chipotle, shut down, or to already be a beloved neighborhood institution and therefore teeter on until the owners decide to retire. I believe the /Map/ apps exacerbate the sameness, but it would be a mistake to say things are all the same because of the review-wall.

I grew up in Austin, TX, and without any personal bias it is obvious to me the greatest restaurant to ever bless the earth was Magnolia Cafe on Lake Austin Boulevard. The original Magnolia cafe is still on South Congress, though the Lake Austin establishment had home field advantage for me and my fellow West Austinites. It was a 24-hour establishment, had an Escher-like physical layout, and every square inch of touchabel surface was sticky with syrup or some syrup-like residue. The waitstaff did not care if you lived or died, and so tolerated my high school friends, first responders, and meandering vagabonds all the same. The food was good-to-mediocre but plentiful, and I never got the sense anyone left unsatisfied with their meal. Eagle-eyed readers may notice I’m writing in the past tense here, as Lake Austin Mag’s closed in April during the first year of COVID. I wonder if Magnolia could be brought back from the ashes.

Mag’s has since been replaced with a fried chicken chain that is simultaneously delicious and void of a tenth of the heart of it’s ancestral tenant. When I am home, and drive by what used to be Mag’s, the chicken place is packed to the gills with happy customers. So be it, to rail against the existence of trends is an exercise in futility. The \Maps\ reviews of the chicken place are glowing.

To live by the review need not be a requirement. During a summer in college between junior and senior years, I spent time in Alabama on a bird refuge along the Georgia/Alabama state line. The towns along the Chattahoochee corridor were immune  the review-wall. In its place was the parking-wall, which was a tecnique bestowed upon me by a rather portly young man I had shadow me at work. He told me:

> If you don’t know where to eat, at lunch or dinner time just get in your car and drive around until you find the fullest parking lot you can see, then stop

The technique is not so different than the \Map\ app reviews in a way. To see a full parking lot is an invitation to immerse your own car in the revelry of others.

To be online is to interact with, to various degrees, Reddit. Comments on reddit rise to the top based on the upvote. By default, the comments you see on a post bubble up the most “upvoted” comments, or the highest ratio of “upvote/downvote” (downvote, being the inverse of upvoting). An upvote is a value judgement assigned to a comment. Our forums are based around this concept, where the discussion surfaces the most highly reviewed opinions first. The cream rises to the top. What do we get from this system? A sameness of opinion, a weaving of stories that is only true in the median. The real honest kernals of truth are hidden in the edges where the reality is harder and more opaque. The hard realities are confusing and unpopular. What is popular is easy to digest. And what is easy to digest is what we see copied and pasted ad nausieum.

But again I ask, what is so valuable about the copy and pasted judgements, reviews, and opinions of us all where we are only given such a minisclue sliver of the real meat that what we can posit and post is just hot air? The answer is of course there is nothing valuable to reviews and comments. Listen to your eyes and ears only, believe it if you can see it.
