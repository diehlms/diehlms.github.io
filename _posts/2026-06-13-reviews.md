I was talking with my parents a couple of years ago about a promotional feature a now defunct Airline presented frequent fliers. For a reasonable cost, you could purchase a vacation package which included hotel stays, return airline trips, and one or two other nice to haves with the caveat being you, the purchaser of the package, will not know where you are going until you show up to the airport for your flight. I am not sure how much nudging the company would give you in terms of what to pack, what to expect, or if there were any opt-outs with regards to cities which you may be attempting to never go back to.
<br/><br/>
My personality dictates me to a maximum of 15 minutes of research on any given topic, and therefore I have no interesting data points in which to present on the surprise Airline vacation package, or any following trends I will write on here.
<br/><br/>
With the previous disclaimer out of the way, I would posit these packages are minimally popular these days, even though the concept of jetting off to somewhere, anywhere, is such a romantic and instagram-able bit of consumer behavior it remains the only scene in “Yes Man” starring Jim Carry that I remember. My experience (vibe research) tells me the typical interaction with travel, discourse, and the day to day behaviorisms of of consumption is behind of, or in front of, some kind of “review-wall”.  The “review-wall” is a shield which protects and contains against any sort of experimentation with our local entities. The “review-wall” is a muzzle disguised as a megaphone, and through no fault of anyone in particular is likely driving most people just a little mad.
<br/><br/>
The easiest mental ingress to understanding the “review-wall” is likely Google Maps (or Apple Maps, etc). If you have just arrived on Earth and have yet to interact with any of the major map apps and are proficient enough in english to get to this section of the essay, the \* Map apps strive to give travelers, whether they be pedestrians, motorists, or transiters, turn by turn directions to the route of their choosing as well as a forum for any conceivable landmark along the way. These forums are completely public and anyone with an opinion is invited to blast their musings out to the world. If a cafe serves you, a potential reviewer, the greatest latte you’ve ever enjoyed, you are often encouraged to post your review on the \* Map app of your choosing, so other latte enthusiasts can share the experience you’ve just had. Likewise, if you are the pacific ocean, you may be the unwitting recipient of a bad \* Map app review because you, being a body of water, are too cold, wet, and ill tempered for a forum contributor. Review sites like the \* Map apps, and Yelp before them, are outstandingly valuable resources for small businesses, which thrive and perish on the backs of upswells of good or bad reviews; that is to say, the “review-wall” is a valuable service with tangible impact on the way we interact with our communities.
<br/><br/>
However, with time my daily experiences have led me to sense a sameness so many places converge on. Given the brutal financial realities of running a restaurant (that I’ve read about/imagined), the inevitable outcome of a restaurant is to become Chipotle, close, or to already be a beloved neighborhood institution, and therefore teeter on until the owners decide to retire. A reasonable bet before going in to a surface-floor coffee shop of a new mixed-use five-over-one (or the commercial equivalent) is to assume there will be exposed ducts and ventilation systems, whereas ten years ago the various subcomponents of the HVAC system would have been patched over with a shallow ceiling. But Chipotle interiors don’t cover the HVAC system, and people like Chipotle. Repeat ad nausiem with QR code menus, braised brussel sprout starters, white-labelled dog treats. It is not suffice to say trendy elements are bad, as I love brussel sprouts. In the instance of the QR code menus, COVID bore them necessary so as to contain the spread of germs; it just so happens not having to purchase paper menu shores up a non-negligible amount of spending better suited elsewhere. Moreover, restaurants more than other businesses, are by necessity required to follow trend lines wherever they may lead. But the */ Map apps exacerbate the sameness. To try a novel approach is to tempt fate with a three, two, or god save you, one star reviews. To survive on margins which leave no air to breath, the sameness must be the salvation for month over month survival. But to me, I find it hard to imagine being great within the spirit of sameness.
<br/><br/>
I grew up in Austin, TX, and without any personal bias it is obvious to me that the greatest restaurant to ever bless the earth was Magnolia Cafe on Lake Austin Boulevard. The original Magnolia cafe is still on South Congress, though the Lake Austin establishment had home field advantage for my West Austin compatriots. It was a 24-hour establishment, had an Escher-like geographic layout, and every square inch of surface was sticky with syrup or some syrup like residue. The waitstaff did not care if you lived or died, and so tolerated my high school friends, first responders, and meandering vagabonds all the same. The food was mediocre but plentiful, but I never got the sense anyone left unsatisfied with their meal. Eagle-eyed readers may notice I’m writing in the past tense here, as Lake Austin Mag’s closed in April during the first year of COVID.  As a pure hypothetical, it is a painful but honest truth to say Magnolia’s could not be brought back from the ashes. Hapless patrons may leave chided by the decor or the hospitality and enact their revenge with lethal two or three stars. It is simply impossible to know.
<br/><br/>
Mag’s has since been replaced with a fried chicken chain that is simultaneously delicious and void of a tenth of the heart of it’s ancestral tenant. When I am home, and drive by what used to be Mag’s, the chicken place is packed to the gills with happy customers. So be it, to rail against the existence of trends is an exercise in futility. The \* Maps reviews of the chicken place are glowing; the few negative reviews are juvenile and at some point inevitable once a business has reached terminal velocity.
<br/><br/>
To live by the review need not be a requirement. During a summer in college between junior and senior years, I spent time in Alabama on a bird refuge along the Georgia/Alabama state line. The towns along the Chattahoochee corridor were immune to the review-wall. In its place was the parking-wall, which was a term bestowed upon me by a rather portly young man I had shadow me at work. He told me, paraphrasing:
<br/><br/>
If you don’t know where to eat, at lunch or dinner time just get in your car and drive around until you ginf the fullest parking lot you can see, then stop there
<br/><br/>
The technique is not so different than the \* Map app reviews in a way, but to me serves as a pull in, not a push out. To see a full parking lot is an invitation to immerse your own car in the revelry of other eaters.
<br/><br/>
To hark on restaurants being popular because a lot of people like things feels incredibly unserious, and unlikely to make me a man-about-time through my cultural musings. I wish to say the “review-wall” makes it’s appearance known in larger, darker ways as well.
<br/><br/>
To be online is to interact with, to various degrees, Reddit. I will be brief here: Reddit is a large online community aggregator, where communities (subreddits) feed into one surface layer which can be customized to a user’s preference. Subreddits range from global, country, city, or neighborhood scales and represent nearly all of the various permutations of the human experience and interests. Comments are ubiquitous and are an opt-out feature on posts.
<br/><br/>
Comments rise to the top based on the upvote. By default, the comments you see on a post bubble up the most “upvoted” comments, or the highest ratio of “upvote/downvote” (downvote, being the inverse of upvoting). An upvote, or downvote, is a value judgement assigned to a comment. If comments are downvoted past a certain threshold, they cease to be automatically displayed on the page and must be manually opened. However, there are no rules to what should be upvoted or downvoted. The typical outwardly racist comment will likely be downvoted, unless of course it is on a racism-forward subreddit. A typical provocative, edgy comment that subverts the narrative of a post will have a mix of upvote and downvotes; these can be sorted by “controversial” tags. A typical framing of the topic that matches the typical reader’s expectation and already-held viewpoint will be upvoted.
<br/><br/>
I read comments mostly before reading an article (if I even read the article at all), and I detest the commenting system. I have no suggested improvement to make. The comment vote-sorting system is a flattening of discourse; it serves only to amplify the opinions that are already held by the majority of readership. This is the case on major newspaper commenting systems, where comments are given little thumbs-up aggregation scores, though they are less outwardly sorted in the UI by number of “upvotes”.
